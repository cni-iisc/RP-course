# Law of Total Probability

:::: tcolorbox
::: {#exerc:CountableCoinToss .exerc}
**Exercise 1** (Countably infinite coin tosses). Consider a sequence of
coin tosses, such that the sample space is
$\Omega = \left\{H,T\right\}^\mathbb{N}$. For set of outcomes
$E_n \triangleq \left\{\omega \in \Omega: \omega_n = H\right\}$, we
consider an event space generated by
$\mathcal{F}\triangleq \sigma(\left\{E_n: n\in \mathbb{N}\right\})$. Let
$\mathcal{F}_n$ be the event space generate by the first $n$ coin
tosses, i.e.
$\mathcal{F}_n \triangleq \sigma(\left\{E_i: i \in [n]\right\})$. Let
$A_n$ be the set of outcomes corresponding to at least one head in first
$n$ outcomes
$A_n \triangleq \left\{\omega \in \Omega: \omega_i = H \text{ for some } i \in [n]\right\} = \cup_{i=1}^nE_i \in \mathcal{F},$
and $B_n$ be the set of outcomes corresponding to first head at the
$n$th outcome
$B_n \triangleq \left\{\omega \in \Omega: \omega_1 = \dots = \omega_{n-1}= T, \omega = H\right\} 
= \cap_{i=1}^{n-1}E_i^c\cap E_n \in \mathcal{F}.$

1.  Show that
    $\mathcal{F}= \sigma(\left\{\mathcal{F}_n: n \in \mathbb{N}\right\})$.

2.  Show that
    $\sigma(\left\{A_n: n \in \mathbb{N}\right\}) \subseteq \mathcal{F}$
    and
    $\sigma(\left\{B_n: n\in \mathbb{N}\right\}) \subseteq \mathcal{F}$.
:::
::::

::: thm
**Theorem 2** (Law of total probability). *For a probability space
$(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)$, consider a sequence of events
$B\in \mathcal{F}^\mathbb{N}$ that partitions the sample space $\Omega$,
i.e. $B_m \cap B_n = \emptyset$ for all $m \neq n$, and
$\cup_{n \in \mathbb{N}}B_n = \Omega$. Then, for any event
$A \in \mathcal{F}$, we have
$$P(A) = \sum_{n \in \mathbb{N}}P(A \cap B_n).$$*
:::

::: proof
We can expand any event $A \in \mathcal{F}$ in terms of any
partition $B$ of the sample space $\Omega$ as
$$A = A \cap \Omega = A \cap (\cup_{n \in \mathbb{N}}B_n) = \cup_{n \in \mathbb{N}}(A \cap B_n).$$
From the mutual disjointness of the events
$B \in \mathcal{F}^\mathbb{N}$, it follows that the sequence
$(A \cap B_n \in \mathcal{F}: n \in \mathbb{N})$ is mutually disjoint.
The result follows from the countable additivity of probability of
disjoint events. ◻
:::

:::: shaded
::: exmp
**Example 3** (Countably infinite coin tosses). Consider the sample
space $\Omega = \left\{H,T\right\}^\mathbb{N}$ and event space
$\mathcal{F}$ generated by sequence $E \in \mathcal{F}^\mathbb{N}$
defined in Exercise [1](#exerc:CountableCoinToss){reference-type="ref"
reference="exerc:CountableCoinToss"}. We observe that any event
$A \in \mathcal{F}_n$ can be written as
$$A = \cup_{\omega \in A}\left\{\omega\right\} 
= \cup_{\omega \in A}\cap_{i=1}^n(\left\{\omega \in E_i\right\}\cup\left\{\omega \notin E_i\right\}).$$
:::

# Independence

::: defn
**Definition 4** (Independence of events). For a probability space
$(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)$, a family of events $A \in \mathcal{F}^I$ is
said to be independent, if for any finite set $F \subseteq I$, we have
$$P(\cap_{i \in F}A_i) = \prod_{i \in F}P(A_i).$$
:::

::: rem
*Remark 1*. The certain event $\Omega$ and the impossible event
$\emptyset$ are always independent to every event $A \in \mathcal{F}$.
:::

:::: shaded
::: exmp
**Example 5** (Two coin tosses). Consider two coin tosses, such that the
sample space is $\Omega = \left\{HH, HT, TH, TT\right\}$, and the event
space is $\mathcal{F}= \mathcal{P}(\Omega)$. It suffices to define a
probability function $P: \mathcal{F}\to [0,1]$ on the sample space. We
define one such probability function $P$, such that
$$P(\left\{HH\right\}) = P(\left\{HT\right\}) = P(\left\{TH\right\}) =P(\left\{TT\right\}) = \frac{1}{4}.$$
Let event $E_1 \triangleq \left\{HH, HT\right\}$ and
$E_2 \triangleq \left\{HH, TH\right\}$ correspond to getting a head on
the first or the second toss respectively.

From the defined probability function, we obtain the probability of
getting a tail on the first or the second toss is $\frac{1}{2}$, and
identical to the probability of getting a head on the first or the
second toss. That is, $P(E_1) = P(E_2) = \frac{1}{2}$ and the
intersecting event $E_1\cap E_2 = \left\{HH\right\}$ with the
probability $P(E_1 \cap E_2) = \frac{1}{4}$. That is, for events
$E_1, E_2 \in \mathcal{F}$, we have $$P(E_1 \cap E_2) = P(E_1)P(E_2).$$
That is, events $E_1$ and $E_2$ are independent.
:::
::::

:::: shaded
::: exmp
**Example 6** (Countably infinite coin tosses). Consider the outcome
space $\Omega = \left\{H,T\right\}^\mathbb{N}$ and event space
$\mathcal{F}$ generated by the sequence $E$ defined in
Exercise [1](#exerc:CountableCoinToss){reference-type="ref"
reference="exerc:CountableCoinToss"}. We define a probability function
$P:\mathcal{F}\to [0,1]$ by
$P(\cap_{i\in F}E_i)= p^{\left\lvert F\right\rvert}$ for any finite
subset $F \subseteq \mathbb{N}$. By definition,
$E \in \mathcal{F}^\mathbb{N}$ is a sequence of independent events.
Consider $A, B \in \mathcal{F}^\mathbb{N}$, where
$A_n \triangleq \cup_{i=1}^nE_i$ and
$B_n \triangleq \cap_{i=1}^{n-1}E_i^c\cap E_n \in \mathcal{F}$ for all
$n\in\mathbb{N}$. It follows that $P(A_n) = 1-(1-p)^n$ and
$P(B_n) = p(1-p)^{n-1}$ for $n \in \mathbb{N}$.

For any $\omega \in \Omega$, we can define the number of heads in first
$n$ trials by
$$
k_n(\omega) \triangleq \sum_{i=1}^n \text{\Bbb{1}}_{\left\{H\right\}}(\omega_i)
= \sum_{i=1}^n \text{\Bbb{1}}_{\left\{\omega \in E_i\right\}}.
$$

For any general event
$A \in \mathcal{F}_n = \sigma(\left\{E_i: i \in [n]\right\})$, we can
write
$$P(A) = \sum_{\omega \in A} \prod_{i=1}^n\Big[P\left\{\omega \in E_i\right\}  + P\left\{\omega \in E_i^c\right\}\Big] 
= \sum_{\omega \in A}p^{k_n(\omega)}(1-p)^{n-k_n(\omega)}.$$
:::
::::

::::: shaded
:::: exmp
**Example 7** (Counter example). Consider a probability space
$(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)$ and the events
$A_1, A_2, A_3 \in \mathcal{F}$. The condition
$P(A_1\cap A_2\cap A_3) = P(A_1)P(A_2)P(A_3)$ is not sufficient to
guarantee independence of the three events. In particular, we see that
if

$$
\begin{align*}
    &P(A_1 \cap A_2 \cap A_3) = P(A_1) P(A_2) P(A_3), \\
    &P(A_1 \cap A_2 \cap A_3^c) \neq P(A_1) P(A_2) P(A_3^c),
\end{align*}
$$

then
$P(A_1\cap A_2) =  P(A_1\cap A_2\cap A_3) + P(A_1\cap A_2\cap A_3^c) \neq P(A_1)P(A_2)$.
::::
:::::

::: defn
**Definition 8**. A family of collections of events
$(\mathcal{A}_i \subseteq \mathcal{F}: i \in I)$ is called independent,
if for any finite set $F \subseteq I$ and $A_i \in \mathcal{A}_i$ for
all $i \in F$, we have $$P(\cap_{i \in F}A_i) = \prod_{i \in F}P(A_i).$$
:::

# Conditional Probability

Consider $N$ trials of a random experiment over an outcome space
$\Omega$ and an event space $\mathcal{F}$. Let $\omega_n \in \Omega$
denote the outcome of the experiment of the $n$th trial. Consider two
events $A, B \in \mathcal{F}$ and denote the number of times event $A$
and event $B$ occurs by $N(A)$ and $N(B)$ respectively. We denote the
number of times both events $A$ and $B$ occurred by $N(A\cap B)$. Then,
we can write these numbers in terms of indicator functions as

$$
\begin{align*}
    &N(A) = \sum_{n=1}^N \text{\Bbb{1}}_{\left\{\omega_n \in A\right\}}, \\
    &N(B) = \sum_{n=1}^N \text{\Bbb{1}}_{\left\{\omega_n \in B\right\}}, \\
    &N(A \cap B) = \sum_{n=1}^N \text{\Bbb{1}}_{\left\{\omega_n \in A \cap B\right\}}.
\end{align*}
$$

We denote the relative frequency of events $A, B, A\cap B$ in $N$ trials
by $\frac{N(A)}{N}, \frac{N(B)}{N}, \frac{N(A\cap B)}{N}$ respectively.
We can find the relative frequency of events $A$, on the trials where
$B$ occurred as
$$\frac{\frac{N(A\cap B)}{N}}{\frac{N(B)}{N}} = \frac{N(A \cap B)}{N(B)}.$$
Inspired by the relative frequency, we define the conditional
probability function conditioned on events.

::: defn
**Definition 9**. Fix an event $B \in \mathcal{F}$ such that $P(B) > 0$,
we can define the conditional probability
$P(\cdot | B): \mathcal{F}\to [0,1]$ of any event $A \in \mathcal{F}$
conditioned on the event $B$ as $$P(A|B) = \frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}.$$
:::

::: lem
**Lemma 10** (Conditional probability). *For any event
$B \in \mathcal{F}$ such that $P(B) > 0$, the conditional probability
$P(\cdot | B): \mathcal{F}\to [0,1]$ is a probability measure on space
$(\Omega, \mathcal{F})$.*
:::

::: proof
We will show that the conditional probability satisfies all
three axioms of a probability measure.

1.  For all events $A \in \mathcal{F}$, we have $P(A|B) \geqslant 0$
    since $P(A \cap B) \geqslant 0$.

2.  For an infinite sequence of mutually disjoint events
    $(A_i \in \mathcal{F}: i \in \mathbb{N})$ such that
    $A_i \cap A_j = \emptyset$ for all $i \neq j$, we have
    $P(\cup_{i \in \mathbb{N}}A_i|B) = \sum_{i \in \mathbb{N}}P(A_i|B)$.
    This follows from disjointness of the sequence
    $(A_i\cap B \in \mathcal{F}: i \in \mathbb{N})$.

3.  Since $\Omega \cap B = B$, we have $P(\Omega |B) = 1$.

 ◻
:::

::: rem
*Remark 2*. For two independent events $A, B \in \mathcal{F}$ such that
$P(A\cap B) > 0$, we have $P(A|B) = P(A)$ and $P(B|A) = P(B)$. If either
$P(A) = 0$ or $P(B) = 0$, then $P(A \cap B) = 0$.
:::

::: rem
*Remark 3*. For any partition $B$ of the sample space $\Omega$, if
$P(B_n) > 0$ for all $n \in \mathbb{N}$, then from the law of total
probability and the definition of conditional probability, we have
$$P(A) = \sum_{n \in \mathbb{N}}P(A|B_n)P(B_n).$$
:::

# Conditional Independence

::: defn
**Definition 11** (Conditional independence of events). For a
probability space $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)$, a family of events
$A \in \mathcal{F}^I$ is said to be conditionally independent given an
event $C \in \mathcal{F}$ such that $P(C) > 0$, if for any finite set
$F \subseteq I$, we have
$$P(\cap_{i \in F}A_i|C) = \prod_{i \in F}P(A_i|C).$$
:::

::: rem
*Remark 4*. Let $C \in \mathcal{F}$ be an event such that $P(C) > 0$.
Two events $A, B \in \mathcal{F}$ are said to be conditionally
independent given event $C$, if $$P(A \cap B|C) = P(A|C) P(B|C).$$ If
the event $C = \Omega$, it implies that $A, B$ are independent events.
:::

::: rem
*Remark 5*. Two events may be independent, but not conditionally
independent and vice versa.
:::

::::: shaded
::: exmp
**Example 12**. Consider two independent events $A, B \in \mathcal{F}$
such that $P(A\cap B) > 0$ and $P(A \cup B) < 1$. Then the events $A$
and $B$ are not conditionally independent given $A\cup B$. To see this,
we observe that
$$P(A \cap B | A\cup B) = \frac{P( (A \cap B)\cap (A \cup B))}{P(A\cup B)} = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(A\cup B)} 
= \frac{P(A)P(B)}{P(A \cup B)} =  {P(A|A \cup B)}{P(B)}.$$ We further
observe that $P(B|A\cup B) = \frac{P(B)}{P(A\cup B)} \neq P(B)$ and
hence $P(A \cap B | A\cup B) \neq P(A|A\cup B)P(B|A\cup B)$.
:::

::: exmp
**Example 13**. Consider two non-independent events
$A, B \in \mathcal{F}$ such that $P(A) > 0$. Then the events $A$ and $B$
are conditionally independent given $A$. To see this, we observe that
$$P(A \cap B | A) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(A)} = P(B|A)P(A|A).$$
:::
